Today I’m continuing my short series “Notes from an Associate Editor”, sharing reflections on how scientific publishing actually works, at least from my own experience.

Artificial intelligence is already part of the scientific publishing ecosystem, and this is becoming increasingly clear.

Some editorial systems are already integrating these tools directly into the workflow.

From the editorial side, AI can be quite helpful. It can assist in identifying potential reviewers, checking for inconsistencies, or highlighting sections that may require closer attention. It is particularly useful for flagging potential issues much faster than manual checking.

In the systems I currently use, tools like AIRA are starting to support parts of the editorial process. For example, they can automatically flag potential issues related to data availability, language quality, text overlap, image integrity, or even scope mismatches. In my view, these tools are most useful when they act as assistants, not decision-makers.

At the same time, I’m increasingly noticing patterns in some manuscripts that suggest a poor or uncritical use of AI tools. Not in all manuscripts, but it is clearly becoming more frequent. This is not about detecting AI itself, but about identifying inconsistencies, superficial reasoning, or text that does not fully align with the data and methods presented.

Authors and reviewers must understand that AI does not replace scientific judgment. For example, this text reflects my own reasoning, but it was organized and polished with the help of a locally installed AI tool.

Decisions about novelty, methodological robustness, or whether conclusions are actually supported by the data still require human evaluation. Context matters, and this is something AI still struggles with.
For authors and reviewers, a few points are worth keeping in mind:

• use AI as a support tool, not as a shortcut, and be transparent about it in your manuscript;
• reviewers should focus on careful reading and interpretation; AI may help organize comments, but not replace critical evaluation;
• ensure that data and methods remain transparent - this is something AI cannot compensate for;

AI is changing the way we interact with scientific publishing, but the responsibility for scientific integrity remains entirely human.
