Today I’m continuing my short series “Notes from an Associate Editor” (#NotesFromAnAssociateEditor), sharing reflections on how scientific publishing actually works, at least from my own experience. Some of the points below are common submission mistakes in the usual editorial sense, while others also reflect my personal view of what should count as a mistake before submitting a manuscript.

The most common submission mistakes

Many authors assume that the most common submission mistakes are mostly technical or formatting-related. In my experience, the most important ones appear earlier, in how the science is presented, structured, and supported.

Some common examples:

• Scope mismatch. A manuscript may be solid, but still not fit the journal or section.

• Misalignment across the paper. The title, abstract, methods, results, and conclusions do not fully match. Sometimes the paper promises one thing, delivers something narrower, and concludes something broader than the data actually support.

• Overstatement. Conclusions are often stronger than the evidence allows. Closely related to this, methods are sometimes described in a way that sounds more powerful or impressive than what was really demonstrated.

• Lack of proportionality. Sometimes methods are not clear enough for proper evaluation. In other cases, the issue is not lack of detail, but poor balance between the method, the data, and the claims being made.

• Limited data transparency. In my personal view, it is a mistake not to share the underlying data whenever possible. Following the FAIR principles makes data more useful to others and strengthens trust, reproducibility, and scientific value.

• Poor internal balance. Sometimes the introduction is too long, while results and discussion are surprisingly short.

• AI-polished but not scientifically filtered writing. It is becoming easier to improve grammar with AI tools, but much harder to know whether the reasoning has been filtered with the same care. A smoother text is not necessarily a stronger manuscript.

Most of these mistakes do not mean the research has no value. But they do make editorial assessment and peer review harder, and they often create doubts that could have been avoided before submission.

In many cases, a stronger submission is not the one that sounds more sophisticated. It is the one that is clearer, better balanced, more transparent, and more faithful to what the data actually show.
