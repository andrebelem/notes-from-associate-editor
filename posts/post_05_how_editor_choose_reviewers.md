Today I’m continuing my short series “Notes from an Associate Editor” (#NotesFromAnAssociateEditor), where I share reflections on how scientific publishing works (and on the wider scientific ecosystem behind it) at least from my own experience. But today I want to write a little differently, and a little more personally. 

Recent experiences reminded me that the lack of transparency is not limited to journal peer review. It also appears in other forms of academic evaluation, where anonymous judgments can be decisive, yet not always sufficiently clear or accountable. Having just read the PNAS opinion by Moles et al. (https://www.pnas.org/doi/epdf/10.1073/pnas.2506681123), I was struck by how their concerns about the sustainability and transparency of peer review resonate far beyond journals. It is also part of our scientific ecosystem, and perhaps one of its darker sides. 

But, one of the least visible parts of editorial work is choosing reviewers. It may look like a routine administrative step, but it is actually one of the moments where editors try to protect fairness, competence, and balance in a system that is often far less transparent than we like to admit. 

Of course, this does not solve the whole problem. Even if editors know who the reviewers are, while authors do not, we can never be entirely sure that no competing interests are at play. At times, a poor or unnecessarily hostile review may suggest that something remains hidden. But a vague, superficial, and overly positive review can be just as concerning, because it may also conceal a competing interest that the process does not easily reveal.

We would like to believe that each of us will take the ethical path, openly declare when we are not in a position to review something fairly, and step aside when necessary. I have done that myself more than once. But it would be naïve to assume that the system always works that way. We are human, after all. 

If you have ever received a bad review, try not to take it entirely personally. That is not always easy, of course. But part of academic life is learning to separate genuine criticism, which can help improve your work, from comments that are simply unfair, hostile, or discouraging. The reverse is also true. If a plainly positive review comes wrapped in hidden interests, it is not really a gift. Sooner or later, science has a way of returning that fragility to those who accepted it. 

We should be honest about the darker sides of the system, but we should not let them define our future. There is still a much larger universe of opportunities ahead, and that is always worth remembering.
