Why does open science matter?

From the editorial side, this is not just an abstract principle or a fashionable expression. Open science matters because scientific claims become much stronger when data, methods, and materials follow the FAIR principles: findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable, making them easier to examine, verify, and build upon.

As editors and reviewers, we are constantly trying to assess whether the conclusions of a manuscript are actually supported by the evidence presented. This becomes much easier, and much fairer, when the underlying data are available in a clear and responsible way.

This is one reason why data availability policies are so important. In most serious scientific journals, authors are expected to provide a data availability statement for all published articles, and when a manuscript includes required data types, these data should be made available in public, stable, community-supported repositories whenever possible.

This is not bureaucracy for its own sake.

Good data availability practices improve reproducibility, make peer review more meaningful, and increase confidence in published work. They also allow other researchers to build on existing findings instead of working in isolation or trying to reconstruct what was done from incomplete descriptions.

Of course, openness is not always simple. There are legitimate exceptions involving ethical restrictions, legal limitations, or third-party controlled datasets. But even in these cases, “transparency” still matters. If data cannot be shared publicly, the manuscript should explain why and indicate how access may be managed where appropriate.
In practice, open science helps us move from “trust me” to “here is the evidence.”

For authors, this means that data sharing should be seen as part of the scientific contribution, not as an optional afterthought.

For reviewers, it means paying attention not only to the narrative of the manuscript, but also to whether the evidence is actually accessible and traceable.

For editors, it means recognizing that transparency is not a minor technical issue, but part of scientific rigor.
Open science does not solve every problem in publishing. But it makes science more accountable, more reusable, and ultimately more credible.

That is why open science matters.
